Good. Here is the word for the day. Dramatic irony: the reader knows something a character does not.

Golding shows you the figure on the mountain before any boy sees it. So when the twins say they saw the beast with their own eyes, you hear two stories at once: theirs, and the true one.

Part 1Chapter Thinking
1. At the castle Ralph says, “I’m chief. I’ll go. Don’t argue.” (Ch. 6). Earlier on the walk he was “thankful to have escaped responsibility for a time” (Ch. 6). Why does he go first anyway, and what does it cost him?
2. After the argument, Piggy struggles for breath, and Golding writes, “Nobody minded him.” (Ch. 6). What does that short sentence show about the group, and about Ralph?
3. Ralph tries to hold on to one word (“Smoke.”) and then “Something flittered there in front of his mind like a bat’s wing, obscuring his idea.” (Ch. 6). What is happening to Ralph’s thinking, and why does it matter for the boys?
4. At the end of Chapter 5, Ralph wishes, “If only they could send us something grownup.” (Ch. 5). The next chapter opens, “But a sign came down from the world of grownups” (Ch. 6). What sign comes, and what might Golding be saying about the grownups’ world?

The moment: In Chapter 5, Jack shakes Percival and asks about the beast without the conch. Golding writes that Jack “had not got the conch and thus spoke against the rules; but nobody minded” (Ch. 5).

One word or action: “nobody minded.” The rule gets broken in front of everybody and it doesn’t even matter to them. The conch only works if people care.

Working claim: Jack doesn’t fight the conch here, he just talks without it, and that is worse because nobody stops him. Closing idea: when order gets weak, Jack gets louder.
