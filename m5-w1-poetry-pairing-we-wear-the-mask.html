The Face We Show, and What It Covers

🌟 ABOUT THIS PAGE
Short, optional reading. Nothing on this page is graded. About fifteen minutes; pairs a short poem with the week’s story.
📖 FOCUS
A calm face does not always mean a calm heart.
Paul Laurence Dunbar published "We Wear the Mask" in 1895, in a collection called Majors and Minors. Dunbar was born in Dayton, Ohio, to parents who had once been enslaved in Kentucky, and he became one of the first American poets of African descent to win wide national recognition in the years after the Civil War. His poem describes a mask that is not a real object but a performance: a face arranged to grin and reassure, kept in place by someone who is actually suffering underneath it. The mask lets its wearer move through an ordinary day among other people without ever letting on what is truly felt. Its central image, a calm face kept up in front of others while something else goes on underneath, connects to the village in "The Lottery."
🎯 LESSON OBJECTIVES
1
I will be able to read Dunbar's poem and describe what the mask hides and why the speaker keeps it on.
2
I will be able to connect the poem's idea of a hidden feeling behind a calm performance to a text I am about to read.
📋 MATERIALS
▶
The poem (printed below), writing notebook, and about fifteen minutes.
📖 PART 1 · READ ALOUD
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a time you kept smiling or acting fine in front of other people while you actually felt upset, worried, or unhappy underneath. What made you keep up that front?

Now read the whole poem aloud, slowly. Notice the line that repeats at the end of each stanza.

WE WEAR THE MASK · PAUL LAURENCE DUNBAR (1895)
We wear the mask that grins and lies,
It hides our cheeks and shades our eyes,--
This debt we pay to human guile;
With torn and bleeding hearts we smile,
And mouth with myriad subtleties.

Why should the world be over-wise,
In counting all our tears and sighs?
Nay, let them only see us, while
    We wear the mask.

We smile, but, O great Christ, our cries
To thee from tortured souls arise.
We sing, but oh the clay is vile
Beneath our feet, and long the mile;
But let the world dream otherwise,
    We wear the mask!
This poem is in the public domain.
📝 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

Dunbar says the mask works with "myriad subtleties." Tap to check the meaning, then practice the word several ways.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
myriad
Myriad means an extremely large, hard-to-count number. Dunbar says the mask works through "myriad subtleties," countless small, careful ways of hiding a true feeling behind a pleasant face.
🌱 WORD ROOTS
Myriad comes from the Greek myrioi, meaning "ten thousand," the largest number the ancient Greeks had a single word for. English later borrowed it loosely, for any number too great to easily count, not just the figure ten thousand.
📝 WORD STUDY
Write a sentence using myriad. Then rewrite it twice: once with a synonym, once expanding it ("myriad, in the sense that…").
⚡ PART 3 · WHAT THE MASK REVEALS

Look again at four lines from the poem. For each, choose what it reveals about the speaker's experience of hiding real feeling from the people watching.

⚡ TRY IT · READ THE LINE AGAIN
Use the printed poem above to answer.
1. "We wear the mask that grins and lies, / It hides our cheeks and shades our eyes." What does this reveal?
2. "With torn and bleeding hearts we smile." What does this reveal?
3. "Nay, let them only see us, while / We wear the mask." What does this reveal?
4. "But let the world dream otherwise, / We wear the mask!" What does this reveal?
▶  In your notebook, write one sentence naming what you think is hardest about wearing a mask like this one. Click to compare.
Many readers point to the loneliness of it: the mask works precisely because no one else can tell it is there. The wearer carries the real feeling alone, in the middle of a crowd. Your answer may name something different, such as the exhaustion of keeping it up every day. What matters is that you can point to a line and say why.
💬 PART 4 · A MASK WORN TOGETHER

Dunbar's speaker says "we," not "I." The mask is worn by a whole group of people, together, in front of the world. This week’s story centers on a village that gathers for an old tradition on an ordinary morning, greeting each other, chatting, going through the motions.

⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you meet the villagers in "The Lottery," think about Dunbar's mask, a calm face worn in front of others. What might make a whole town wear a mask like that together?
💪 CONNECTION TO THE STORY

Dunbar's mask is a performance kept up in front of other people, hiding what is actually felt underneath. In "The Lottery," the villagers greet each other, chat, and go through the motions of their tradition. The question is whether every face in that crowd shows what the person underneath actually feels.
