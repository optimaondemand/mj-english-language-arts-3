The Weight of a Single Word
Writing Style
✏️ DICTION: THE SAME EVENT, THREE WORD CHOICES
NEUTRAL
The man walked down the street.
Plain and flat. Tells you nothing about his mood.
CONFIDENT
The man strode down the street.
One verb tells you he is sure of himself.
UNEASY
The man crept down the street.
Now you feel fear or guilt, from one word.
Same street, same man. The diction (the writer’s exact choice of words) does the work.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A writer almost never uses the first word that comes to mind.
Diction is a writer’s choice of words. Two words can point at the same thing and still carry different feelings: a person can be thrifty or stingy, determined or stubborn, quiet or cold. The dictionary meaning may be close, but the feeling each word carries, its connotation, is not. Harper Lee chooses her words with great care. When Scout describes the people of Maycomb, the exact words she reaches for show how the town wants to be seen and how it really behaves. In this week’s chapters Atticus shows what he believes about courage; the words Lee gives him, and the ones she withholds, carry that belief. The skill of diction is choosing the word whose feeling matches the intended meaning, not just its dictionary definition.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I will be able to define diction and explain how connotation differs from dictionary meaning.
2
I will be able to choose a word whose feeling fits the meaning I want a sentence to carry.
📋 MATERIALS
▶
Writing notebook and pencil.
📚 PART 1 · A WORKED EXAMPLE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of two words people might use to describe the same person you know, one kind and one unkind. Did the words point at the same trait?

Here is the test for diction. Pick the word whose feeling matches the meaning you want, even when several words share a dictionary definition. These pairs were written for this lesson.

"He was careful with money" vs. "He was tightfisted with money." → same habit, but the second word judges him. Connotation, not definition, changed.
"She insisted on the truth" vs. "She nagged about the truth." → the second makes a virtue sound like a flaw. Word choice steers the reader.
"The crowd gathered" vs. "The crowd massed." → the second feels heavier, more like a threat. A single verb sets the mood.
⚡ PART 2 · YOUR TURN

Each pair was written for this lesson. The two words share a rough meaning. Which word carries the more negative feeling, the connotation that judges?

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH WORD JUDGES?
In each pair, choose the word with the more negative connotation.
1. A person who will not change his mind.
2. A person who spends very little money.
3. A person who is very sure of himself.
4. People talking easily for a long while.
▶  In your notebook, write one sentence about a person in Maycomb using a neutral word. Then rewrite it twice, once with a kinder word and once with an unkinder word for the same trait. Then click to see an example.
Example: "The neighbor watched the street all day" becomes "The neighbor kept a caring eye on the street" or "The neighbor spied on the street all day." The action is identical; only the diction changed. Answers will differ; check that each version keeps the same event but shifts the feeling.
