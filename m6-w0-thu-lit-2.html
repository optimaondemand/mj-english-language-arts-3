📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A short poem can still make an argument, one stanza building on the last.
Each stanza of “Invictus” adds a new piece to the same claim. The poem opens by naming the dark (stanza 1), then shows the speaker holding steady under attack (stanza 2), then faces the hardest threat of all, the years still to come and what waits beyond them (stanza 3), before landing on its final claim (stanza 4). Reading the stanzas in order, as an argument rather than four separate verses, is what turns this from a nice-sounding poem into a real answer to a real question: what does it cost to remain yourself when your circumstances are not your choosing?
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I will be able to describe what each stanza adds to the poem's overall claim.
2
I will be able to connect the poem's idea of self-mastery to a question the novel you are about to read will also ask.
📋 MATERIALS
▶
Writing notebook and the printed poem from the read-aloud lesson.
📚 PART 1 · A WORKED EXAMPLE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Reread stanza 1 and stanza 4 side by side. Does the speaker's claim get stronger, weaker, or stay exactly the same by the end?

Here is the shape of the argument, stanza by stanza:

Stanza 1: names the dark, then claims an unconquerable soul.
Stanza 2: under attack, he has not winced nor cried aloud.
Stanza 3: facing even the years ahead and what looms beyond them, he will be unafraid.
Stanza 4: whatever the gate or the scroll, he is the master and the captain.
The claim grows from “I have a soul that cannot be conquered” to “I command my own fate,” one stanza at a time.
⚡ PART 2 · YOUR TURN

Match each stanza to what it adds to the poem's argument.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THIS STANZA ADD?
Choose the best answer.
1. What does stanza 3 add that stanzas 1 and 2 do not yet mention?
2. Which stanza makes the poem's strongest, most forceful claim?
