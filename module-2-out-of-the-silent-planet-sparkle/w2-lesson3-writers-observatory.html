Two traps
Ransom ate and listened. One subject, two verbs: still simple, no comma.
Ransom climbed out, Weston followed. Two complete thoughts, only a comma: a comma splice.
Three ways to fix a splice
A joining word: Ransom climbed out, and Weston followed.
A full stop: Ransom climbed out. Weston followed.

2
✏️ Step 1 · Learn
Find the joining word, then name the sentence.

A simple sentence is one independent clause. It can have two verbs for one subject (a compound predicate), and then it takes no comma. A compound sentence joins two independent clauses with a comma and a joining word (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) or with a semicolon. A comma splice joins them with a comma alone. Then and however are not joining words.

📖 Lewis joins

Four sentences from Lewis. Tap each to see what joins it.

“But death could be faced, and rational fear of death could be mastered.” Lewis · Chapter 6
Compound. A comma and and join two complete thoughts. Cover and: both halves stand alone.
“The valley grew steeper, and he came to a little cataract.” Lewis · Chapter 8
Compound. Comma plus and, joining two complete thoughts.
“All was danger; he was no safer travelling than resting.” Lewis · Chapter 8
Compound, joined by a semicolon. No joining word is needed: the semicolon does the joining.
“He found that he had been holding his breath, and breathed deeply again.” Lewis · Chapter 5
Not compound. Cover and: can “breathed deeply again” stand alone? No. One subject, he, has two verbs, found and breathed. Lewis’s comma is a pause, not a join.

🔎 How strong is the imitation?

Here is a model written for this lesson. It is not Lewis. It makes three moves.

At first there was only the hum in his ears. Then the smell of warm metal, sharp and faintly sweet. He opened his eyes to a light too gold to be the sun, and against his will, his fear loosened into astonishment.
  1. The senses come one at a time.
  2. The details are concrete.
  3. The feeling turns at the end.

Draft model

Tess climbed the last of the ridge and stopped. At first the pale shapes ahead looked like a row of broken fence posts. Then they looked like candles. Then they looked like a crowd standing very still. The snow squeaked under her boots, but the shapes made no sound at all. Her helmet smelled of warm plastic and her own breath. She counted forty, and every one stood taller than the lander. Each was turned toward the same dark edge of the sky. They were not posts, and they were not people. Machines, she decided. Somebody built these and left. Far below the ice, something glowed a slow, patient blue. Tess forgot the cold in her fingers. She had come to map a dead moon, and the ridge felt as if it were holding its breath.

The move I borrowed from Lewis: three wrong guesses before the eyes finally settle on an answer.
