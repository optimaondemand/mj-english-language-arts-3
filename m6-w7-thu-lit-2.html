What It Cost, and Who Paid
Literature Lesson
📖 FOCUS
A verdict is read in a minute, but the bill for it is paid over a long time, by many people.
The previous lesson covered what happens; this lesson weighs what it costs. The question of the module is what justice costs in a community that does not yet want it, and what it asks of the few who serve it. These chapters supply evidence toward an answer. Tom Robinson pays one price. Atticus pays another. The children pay a third, in the way the world looks to them afterward. Even the town pays, though many in it do not see the bill. Some costs are shown directly while others are only suggested. Who is most to blame and what the novel finally argues are questions for later; this lesson traces the costs.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I will be able to name several costs the verdict imposes and who bears each one.
2
I will be able to tell a cost the novel shows directly from one it leaves implied.
📋 MATERIALS
▶
Own copy of To Kill a Mockingbird and writing notebook. Parts of Chapters 21 to 24 are reread.
📖 PART 1 · COUNT THE COSTS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a time someone did the right thing and it cost them something anyway. What did it cost?

Skim back through Chapters 21 to 24. In the notebook, list three different people or groups and, beside each, the price the verdict exacts from them.

📝 A NOTE ON READING FOR COST
Some costs are stated plainly on the page. Others are inferred from a small detail: a change in how a character speaks, a silence, a door that closes. Both count as evidence. As the list is made, mark which costs the novel shows outright and which it only lets the reader sense.
⚡ PART 2 · SHOWN OR IMPLIED?

For each cost below, decide whether the novel shows it directly on the page, or leaves it for the reader to infer.

⚡ TRY IT · DIRECT OR IMPLIED?
Does the novel show this cost directly, or leave it implied?
1. The legal outcome handed to Tom Robinson once the jury returns.
2. The private weight the case leaves on Atticus once the trial ends.
3. What the verdict costs the children in how they now see their town.
4. The visible reactions in and around the courtroom in the moments after the verdict.
📝 PART 3 · WORD STUDY

After the verdict, the town hopes the trouble will quietly abate. Tap to check the meaning.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
abate
Abate means to lessen, to die down, to become less intense. After the verdict, some in Maycomb expect the anger to abate and life to return to its usual shape, as though nothing lasting had been paid.
🌱 WORD ROOTS
Abate arrives through Old French from Latin ad, “to,” and battuere, “to beat.” To abate is to beat a thing down until less of it remains. The same battuere strikes battle and combat.
📝 WORD STUDY · PRACTICE
Write a sentence using abate. Then rewrite it twice: once with a synonym, once expanding it ("a tension that would not abate until…"). Look abate up in a dictionary and record three things: how the word breaks into syllables, how it is said aloud, and what part of speech it is.
🖹 PART 4 · THE SAME VERDICT IN TWO MEDIA

A multimodal text carries its meaning through more than one channel at once: words, images, sound, or motion. A printed page uses one. A film uses several.

Each channel can do something the others cannot, and each gives something up. Reading the verdict, the reader supplies the courtroom, and the silence is whatever silence the reader imagines. Watching it, the silence is a specific length, at a specific volume, on a specific face.

🔍 WHAT TO NOTICE
In a printed text, the reader controls pace and supplies the images. In a filmed text, the director controls pace and supplies the images, and adds sound. Ask of any version: what did this form decide, and what did it leave to the audience?
⚡ TRY IT · WHICH FORM IS DOING THIS?
Each effect below is available to one form more than the other. Choose which.
1. The audience sees only what the camera shows; the people at the back stay unseen unless the shot includes them.
2. You can reread one sentence four times before moving on, and nothing about the work changes.
3. A silence lasts precisely eleven seconds, and you cannot hurry it.
4. Every person who experiences it pictures a different face for the same character.
▶  In your notebook: name one thing the printed verdict does that a filmed verdict could not. Then click.
One answer: the novel can stay inside a narrator's head while the room reacts, so you get the event and a private response to it at the same time. Film has to choose between showing the room and showing the face. Yours may differ; the test is whether your answer names a channel the other form does not have.
