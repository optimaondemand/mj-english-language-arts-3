📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A world that has stopped answering to the order that once held it together.
In 1919, the Irish poet William Butler Yeats wrote about a world in which the shared order holding people together seemed to be giving way. The poem, “The Second Coming,” has become one of the most quoted in the English language, largely because of a handful of lines that describe what it looks like when order breaks down and confidence collapses along with it. The same idea plays out on Golding's island in Lord of the Flies.

📖 PART 1 · WHO WROTE THIS, AND WHEN

William Butler Yeats (1865–1939) was an Irish poet, one of the most important writers of the twentieth century. He wrote “The Second Coming” in 1919, months after the end of the First World War, a conflict that had killed millions of soldiers and toppled long-standing governments across Europe. Yeats was also watching violence break out closer to home, as Ireland moved toward its own war for independence from Britain. Writing in that moment, he reached for an image from falconry, the old practice of hunting with a trained bird. A falconer releases a falcon to circle overhead in a widening loop, and calls it back with a whistle or a cry. In the poem, the falcon has circled out so far that it can no longer hear the falconer's call. The bird is still flying, but nothing it does now answers to the one who is supposed to be guiding it. That single image, a falcon that will not come back, is Yeats's picture of a whole world that has stopped answering to the order that once held it together.

The poem's second half turns to a strange, unsettling vision: a shape moving through the desert, which Yeats ties to old ideas about history repeating in great cycles. That part of the poem has puzzled and divided readers for a century, and it is not the focus of this page. What matters here is the poem's clearer opening lines, the ones about order failing and conviction failing along with it, because those are the lines that echo loudest in the novel.

THE SECOND COMING · W.B. YEATS (1920)
Turning and turning in the widening gyre
The falcon cannot hear the falconer;
Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold;
Mere anarchy is loosed upon the world,
The blood-dimmed tide is loosed, and everywhere
The ceremony of innocence is drowned;
The best lack all conviction, while the worst
Are full of passionate intensity.

Surely some revelation is at hand;
Surely the Second Coming is at hand.
The Second Coming! Hardly are those words out
When a vast image out of Spiritus Mundi
Troubles my sight: a waste of desert sand;
A shape with lion body and the head of a man,
A gaze blank and pitiless as the sun,
Is moving its slow thighs, while all about it
Wind shadows of the indignant desert birds.
The darkness drops again but now I know
That twenty centuries of stony sleep
Were vexed to nightmare by a rocking cradle,
And what rough beast, its hour come round at last,
Slouches towards Bethlehem to be born?
This poem was first published in 1920 and is in the public domain.
📝 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

Yeats writes that “mere anarchy is loosed upon the world.” Tap to check what the word means.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
anarchy
Anarchy means the complete absence of government, rule, or order; a state in which no shared authority is left to hold things together. Yeats says anarchy is “loosed,” as if it were an animal let off a leash. The word choice suggests something that was being held back and is now running free.
🌱 WORD ROOTS
Anarchy comes from Greek an-, “without,” joined to arkhos, “ruler.” It means, literally, “without a ruler.” The same root arkh- gives us words like monarchy (rule by one) and hierarchy (rule by rank).

📚 PART 4 · CARRYING THIS INTO THE NOVEL

Lord of the Flies places a group of boys on an island with no adults, no government, and no institutions left standing. Golding asks, in his own way, the same question Yeats asks in this poem.

▶  In your own words, write one or two sentences on what you expect “the centre” to be for the boys on the island: the rule, the person, or the object that is supposed to hold their order together. Click to compare.
Many readers point to the conch and the assembly it calls together as the boys' first stand-in for a shared center: a rule everyone agrees, at first, to answer to. There is no single right answer yet. The point is to watch for what Yeats's poem asks readers to watch for: what holds a group together, and what happens when it stops holding.
