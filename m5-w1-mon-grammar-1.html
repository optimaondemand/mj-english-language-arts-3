The Hint You Only Catch Later
Craft Lesson
📖 FOCUS
A good ending feels both surprising and, somehow, inevitable.
Foreshadowing is a quiet hint, planted early, that prepares the reader for something later in the story. The skill of it is restraint: the hint must be small enough to slip past the reader the first time, yet real enough that, looking back, it was there all along. A pile of stones described in a single calm sentence. A child filling his pockets. A box no one wants to touch. None of these announce danger. Each one waits. When the ending arrives, the buried hints suddenly connect, and the surprise feels earned rather than cheap.
🎯 LESSON OBJECTIVES
1
I will be able to define foreshadowing and explain why it is planted quietly.
2
I will be able to tell buried foreshadowing apart from an open warning.
📋 MATERIALS
▶
Writing notebook and pencil. About 5 to 10 minutes of focused practice.
📚 PART 1 · A WORKED EXAMPLE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a movie or book whose ending made you want to go back to the beginning. What did a second look show you?

Here is the test for buried foreshadowing: a true hint can be read two ways. On the first pass it seems harmless or even pleasant. Only the ending reveals its second meaning. These sentences, written for this lesson, work that way.

"The boys gathered the smoothest, roundest stones and stacked them in a neat pile by the wall." → sounds like ordinary play. Buried hint.
"Mr. Pell ran his thumb along the edge of the worn wooden chest and set it down quickly." → a small uneasy gesture, easy to skip. Buried hint.
"Warning: someone in this village is about to die." → this is not foreshadowing at all. An open announcement, which kills the surprise.
⚡ PART 2 · PRACTICE

Each sentence below was written for this lesson. Is it buried foreshadowing, or an open announcement that gives the ending away?

⚡ TRY IT · BURIED HINT OR OPEN ANNOUNCEMENT?
Choose the best label for each sentence.
1. By the well, a child counted his stones twice and kept the heaviest one.
2. Everyone in town knew the ceremony would end in a killing.
3. The box had grown shabby over the years, but no one was willing to make a new one.
4. The reader should feel dread now, because the gathering will turn deadly.
▶  In your notebook, write one sentence that buries a hint of danger inside an ordinary, calm description. Then click to see an example.
Example: "The new neighbors waved as they unloaded the last box, the one they always carried in two hands." Notice the calm surface and the small odd detail that waits. Your sentence will differ; check that nothing in it openly warns the reader.
