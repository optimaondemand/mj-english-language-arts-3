Who Is Telling This Story?
Writing Style
POINT OF VIEW AT A GLANCE
FIRST PERSON ("I")
One person inside the story tells it. We see only what that person sees and hear only what that person thinks. The camera sits behind one pair of eyes.
Scout uses "I." We are stuck in her head, and that is the point.
THIRD PERSON ("he," "she")
A voice outside the story tells it. It can move from person to person and explain what anyone is thinking. The camera floats above the whole town.
Lee did not choose this.
A child narrator can show us more than she can understand. We see over her shoulder.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A narrator does not just tell a story. A narrator decides what we are allowed to see.
Point of view is the position from which a story is told. The novel is narrated in the first person by Scout, a girl looking back on the years she was six to nine years old. First person means one person inside the story tells it using "I," so the reader knows only what that person knew. Lee makes this choice do real work. Scout often reports things plainly, without grasping their full weight: a remark a neighbor makes, the way adults change the subject, the fear that hangs around the Radley house. Because Scout does not fully understand what she sees, the reader understands it for her. That gap between what the child shows and what the child knows is one of the most important tools in the book.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I will be able to tell first-person narration apart from third-person narration.
2
I will be able to explain why a child narrator can show more than the child understands.

📚 PART 1 · A WORKED EXAMPLE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a time, as a younger child, you saw two adults arguing but did not understand what about. What did you notice anyway?

Here is the test for first person: look for "I" and "we," and ask whether the teller is a person inside the story. Then watch for the second layer: a child reports a thing without judging it, and the reader judges it. These sentences were written for this lesson.

"I did not know why Miss Caroline's face turned red, only that the whole room had gone still." → uses "I," a person inside the story. First person, with a child's limited view.
"She did not know why the room had gone still, but she felt the change." → uses "she," a voice from outside. Third person.
"The town had always treated the matter that way, and everyone understood the reasons." → this knows the whole town's mind. Third person, all-knowing, which a child narrator cannot do.

▶  In your notebook, write one sentence in the first person in which the child narrator shows something she does not fully understand. Then click to see an example.
Example: "I told the new teacher how my reading worked, and I could not see why that made her so unhappy." Notice the plain report and the missing understanding. Answers will differ; check that the child shows a fact but leaves its meaning for the reader to supply.
