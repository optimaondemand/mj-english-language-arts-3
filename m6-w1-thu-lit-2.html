THE FIRST GREAT LESSON OF THE BOOK
SCOUT'S DAY
A frustrating first day: a new teacher who does not understand the town, trouble over her reading, and the Cunninghams and Ewells she cannot make sense of.
→
ATTICUS'S ANSWER
Atticus tells Scout she cannot really understand another person until she tries to see things the way that person does. Read his exact words near the end of Chapter 3 in your own copy.
This is the idea the whole novel will test: can a town learn to do it?
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A child's bad day becomes the moral center of the whole book.
Scout's first day at school goes badly. Her new teacher, fresh to Maycomb, does not understand its families or its ways, and Scout, who can already read, ends up scolded for it. At home, frustrated, Scout wants to quit. Atticus answers with advice that becomes the moral center of the novel: a person cannot really be understood until someone tries to see things the way that person does. Lee lets the lesson arrive through a small, ordinary scene, a hard school day and a talk on the porch, rather than a speech. Because the reader has just shared Scout's confusion, the force of Atticus's idea is felt before it can be fully explained. The central question of the whole module grows out of this idea.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I will be able to explain Atticus's advice about understanding a person from his point of view.
2
I will be able to connect that advice to the module's central question.
📋 MATERIALS
▶
Own copy of To Kill a Mockingbird (Chapters 2 and 3 open) and a writing notebook.
📖 PART 1 · THE LESSON ON THE PORCH
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a time you were sure someone was being unfair, then learned something that changed how you saw it.

Reread the talk between Scout and Atticus at the end of her first school day in your own copy. In your notebook, write in your own words what Atticus is asking Scout to do, and one person from the day he might want her to try it with.

📖 MEMORY PASSAGE
This porch talk holds the first memory-work passage of the module: Atticus's words about considering things from another person's point of view. The text is under copyright, so it is not reproduced here. Learn it from the own copy, and mark its place for later.
⚡ PART 2 · UNDERSTANDING, OR JUST AGREEING?

Atticus is not telling Scout to agree with everyone. He is telling her to understand them first. For each situation written for this lesson, decide whether the person is trying to understand another's point of view, or simply reacting.

⚡ TRY IT · UNDERSTANDING OR JUST REACTING?
Choose the best label for each.
1. A girl is angry a classmate took her seat, then wonders if he could not see well from the back.
2. A boy decides the quiet new family must be unfriendly and stops speaking to them.
3. A student dislikes a new class rule but figures out the worry that made the teacher set it.
📝 PART 3 · WORD STUDY

To consider another person's view fairly takes empathy. Tap to check the meaning, then practice the word several ways.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
empathy
Empathy is the ability to imagine and share another person's feelings, to see a situation as they see it. Atticus is teaching Scout empathy when he asks her to imagine her way into another person's experience.
🌱 WORD ROOTS
Empathy comes from Greek en, “in,” and pathos, “feeling or suffering.” To have empathy is to feel your way inside another person. The same pathos aches in sympathy and pathetic.
📝 WORD STUDY
Write a sentence using empathy. Then rewrite it twice: once with a related word, once expanding it ("an empathy that…"). Look empathy up in a dictionary and record three things: how the word breaks into syllables, how it is said aloud, and what part of speech it is.
