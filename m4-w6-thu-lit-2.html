When the Conch Breaks: Proving a Point
Literature Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A strong claim is only as good as the evidence under it and the reasoning that connects them.
An argument has three parts. The claim is what the writer asserts. The evidence is the fact or quotation that supports it. The warrant is the reasoning that explains why the evidence proves the claim. Many young writers give a claim and a quotation but skip the warrant, so the reader is left to guess how they connect. In Chapter 11 the conch is shattered and Piggy is killed. Piggy is the boy who has stood for reason, for thinking before acting, for the rule of fair talk over force. When he dies and the conch breaks at the same moment, Golding is making a point. That moment can be turned into a tight claim, evidence, and warrant: the skill an essay lives on.
🎯 LESSON OBJECTIVES
1
I will be able to tell a claim, evidence, and a warrant apart.
2
I will be able to write the warrant that links my evidence to my claim.
📋 MATERIALS
▶
Lord of the Flies, Chapter 11 (own copy) and a writing notebook.
📖 PART 1 · READ, THEN BUILD THE ARGUMENT
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Golding lets the conch shatter at the same instant Piggy dies. Why put the two together?

Read Chapter 11 in your own copy. As you read, mark the moment the conch breaks and the moment Piggy falls. They serve as evidence.

📖 YOUR READING
Read Chapter 11 in your copy of Lord of the Flies.

The three parts work together in this example, built for this lesson:

Claim: By Chapter 11, force has fully replaced fair rule on the island.
Evidence: The conch, which gave whoever held it the right to speak, is destroyed at the same moment Piggy is killed.
Warrant: Because the conch stood for the rule of talk over force, breaking it as Piggy dies shows that the boys have stopped settling things by speaking and now settle them by violence.

The warrant does the real work. Without it, the claim and the evidence just sit next to each other. The warrant tells the reader why the broken conch proves the claim.

⚡ PART 2 · CLAIM, EVIDENCE, OR WARRANT?

For each sentence, choose the part of an argument it plays. The sentences are written for this lesson.

⚡ TRY IT · NAME THE PART
Which part of an argument is each sentence?
1. Piggy’s death marks the death of reason on the island.
2. Piggy is the only boy who keeps insisting they hold a meeting and talk it through.
3. Because Piggy alone argues for talking instead of fighting, losing him means the island loses its voice of reason.
📝 PART 3 · WORD STUDY

Piggy keeps appealing to what is rational. Tap to check the meaning.

⚡ TAP TO REVEAL
rational
Rational means guided by reason and clear thinking rather than by feeling or fear. Piggy is the most rational boy on the island, which is exactly why the others stop listening to him.
🌱 WORD ROOTS
Rational comes from Latin ratio, “reckoning or reasoning.” The same root counts in ratio and rate. To be rational is to reckon a thing out.
📝 WORD STUDY · PRACTICE
Write a sentence describing Piggy using rational. Then rewrite it twice: once with a synonym, once expanding it (“rational even when…”). Look rational up in a dictionary and record three things: how the word breaks into syllables, how it is said aloud, and what part of speech it is.
📝 MEMORY WORK

Drill all three passages, and bring the closing lines up to full strength. One passage is recorded for a grade next week.
