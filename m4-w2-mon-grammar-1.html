The Attitude in the Words, the Feeling in the Room
Writing Style Lesson
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A writer controls two feelings at once: the attitude in the words and the atmosphere in the reader.
Tone is the writer's or narrator's attitude toward the subject, the feeling heard in the choice of words: hopeful, uneasy, mocking, grim. Mood is the atmosphere the passage creates in the reader: calm, dread, excitement. The two often work together, but a skilled writer can shift them on purpose. In Chapters 3 and 4 the bright, hopeful island of the first days begins to darken, and Golding moves the tone from cheerful toward tense and uneasy.
🎯 LESSON OBJECTIVES
1
I will be able to tell the difference between tone and mood.
2
I will be able to name the tone or mood a sentence creates.
📋 MATERIALS
▶
Writing notebook and pencil. 5 to 10 minutes.
📚 PART 1 · A WORKED EXAMPLE
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Think of a song that sounds cheerful and a song that sounds tense. What in the music tells you which is which?

Sentences written for this lesson (not from the book) show how the two words work.

“The morning sea sparkled, and the boys laughed as they ran.”
Tone: cheerful, light.   Mood it stirs in the reader: calm and happy.

“The fire had gone out, and the smoke that should have saved them was only a thin grey smear.”
Tone: grim, disappointed.   Mood it stirs in the reader: unease and dread.
⚡ PART 2 · PRACTICE

Each sentence was written for this lesson. Choose the best label for what it mostly does.

⚡ TRY IT · NAME THE FEELING
What does each sentence mostly do?
1. The light thinned over the water, and no one wanted to be the first to speak.
2. The littluns built their small castles of sand, proud as kings of crumbling thrones.
3. They chanted and danced, and the game grew louder until it no longer sounded like a game.
4. In the warm afternoon the leaves barely stirred, and for a while the island seemed kind.
▶  In your notebook, write one sentence about the island with a calm mood, then rewrite it to create an uneasy mood. Click to see an example.
Calm: “The fire glowed softly while the boys slept.”   Uneasy: “The fire guttered low, and the dark crept in around the sleeping boys.” (The mood should actually change.)
